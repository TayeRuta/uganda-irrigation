Where Uganda needs irrigation, and where water is within reach

Almost all of Uganda's farming depends on rain. This report measures, district by district, how often dry spells and failed seasons hit the growing season, whether they actually harm crops, and whether rivers, lakes or groundwater lie within reach of the cropland that needs them. The places that need irrigation most are mostly the places where water is hardest to reach.

Key takeaways

  1. Karamoja faces dry spells far more often than anywhere else. Moroto, Kaabong, Karenga and Amudat have a two-week dry spell in half to three-quarters of growing windows. High
  2. Dry spells cost crops in the North and East, not in the wetter South and West. There a two-week dry spell lowers peak crop greenness by about half a typical year's swing. High
  3. Dry-spell risk has not clearly risen since 1991. It fell a little in Central and rose a little in the East. Medium
  4. 29 districts combine high need with water within reach, through lakes and rivers in Teso, Busoga, Elgon's foothills and around Lake Kyoga, or groundwater in the South-west. They hold 7.6 million people. Medium
  5. 34 high-need districts have no easy water, including almost all of Karamoja. There, water harvesting and drought-tolerant crops fit better than pump irrigation. Medium
  6. The ranking holds when the definitions change, but the size of the list depends on what counts as "within reach": 16 districts under strict rules, 41 under loose ones. High

01 · Seasons

Two rainy seasons in the south, one long one in the north

Each district's 1991–2020 rainfall decides its type. Where the June–August dip falls below 60% of the smaller of the two peaks, the district has two seasons; otherwise one long season. Every district is then measured over two three-month growing windows so that districts compare fairly: March–May and September–November where there are two seasons, April–June and July–September where there is one.

02 · Dry spells and failed seasons

Karamoja is in a different class

Three measures of need, 1991–2025. A long dry spell is 14 or more days in a row with under 2 mm of rain within a growing window. A failed window has under 75% of its usual rain. The water deficit is the water crops would use but cannot get over the growing windows (TerraClimate, 2000–2024).

04 · Water

Is water within reach of the cropland?

Surface water: share of cropland within 1 km of permanent water or of a river with at least 1 m³/s of mean flow. Groundwater: share of the district where boreholes can typically yield 1 litre a second or more, enough for a small solar pump watering 1–2 ha. Flat land: share of cropland on slopes of 3° or less, where fields can be flooded.

Small streams are not mapped at this scale, so surface access is undercounted. Sunlight is ample for solar pumps everywhere.
05 · Where irrigation fits

The greatest need is mostly where water is hardest to reach

Need (0–100) averages each district's rank on dry spells, failed windows and water deficit; the top half is "high need". Water is within reach when at least 10% of cropland lies within 1 km of permanent water or a river, or at least half the district has boreholes yielding 1 litre a second or more. The water score is the better of the two as a multiple of its threshold.

07 · What it means

Three kinds of place, three kinds of answer

High need, water within reach

Pump from lakes, rivers and boreholes

  • Teso, Busoga's lakeshore, Elgon's foothills and the Lake Kyoga shores have flat cropland close to permanent water.
  • Small solar or motorised pumps fit here, and the subsidy programme's targeting could start here.
  • In the South-west cattle corridor groundwater is good but land is hilly: drip and sprinklers suit better than flooding.
High need, water hard to reach

Harvest water and cut risk

  • Almost all of Karamoja and much of the North have little cropland near water and low-yielding boreholes.
  • Valley tanks, small dams and rooftop harvesting store wet-season water.
  • Drought-tolerant crops, early-maturing varieties and dry-spell insurance reduce losses where water cannot be brought in.
Lower need

Irrigate for value, not survival

  • Central and the West rarely lose crops to dry spells.
  • Irrigation there pays through high-value crops (vegetables, fruit) and dry-season production, not drought protection.
  • That is a business case for farmers, not a reason for public targeting.

08 · Methods and limits

How this was done

Method

  • Daily CHIRPS rainfall averaged over each of 135 districts (2020 boundaries), 1991–2025
  • Dry day: under 2 mm; long dry spell: 14+ days within a three-month growing window; failed window: under 75% of the 1991–2020 median
  • Crop response: peak NDVI over cropland (MODIS Terra and Aqua), detrended per district, regressed on window rain or dry spells with errors clustered by year
  • Need: average percentile rank of three measures; water access: fixed thresholds for surface water and groundwater
  • Robustness: ten alternative definitions, each fully recomputed

Limits

  • The cropland map (ESA WorldCover 2021) misses most banana and coffee gardens, so cropland is undercounted in the South and West; 10 districts have too little mapped cropland to score
  • Small streams and swamps are not mapped, so surface access is a lower bound
  • Groundwater maps are continental (5 km); local aquifers can differ
  • District rainfall averages smooth local showers; satellite rainfall misses some local storms
  • FAO's irrigation map dates from about 2005; no public district data exists on the government's micro-irrigation programme
